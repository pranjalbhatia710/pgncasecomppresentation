The case

GTC has to spend $800M on a new routing system without ever going offline.

GTC runs air traffic for 2M flights and 200M passengers a year across 24 countries. It is the only one of Europe's 29 providers that is a public-private partnership, and the EU now requires it to move to trajectory-based routing: 5 years if it goes well, 10 if it doesn't.

Airlines want lower fees, Greenpeace wants less fuel burned, regulators want safety, shareholders want returns, and the Single European Sky lets rivals bid for GTC's airspace.

Our thesis: GTC can turn SES compliance into a competitive advantage and win airline contracts when the market opens, through a phased rollout, a deliberate workforce strategy, and marketing built on measurable results.

Research so far · the PPP model and SES

NATS shows what a PPP buys GTC, and what it costs.

NATS ownership since 2001 [1]
Government 49%Airlines 42%
Plus a government golden share. Staff 5%, Heathrow 4%.
  • NATS, the UK's provider, became a PPP in 2001. The government holds 49% plus a golden share, the Airline Group 42%, staff 5%, Heathrow 4%. [1]
  • Traffic fell after 9/11 and NATS needed refinancing in 2003. A PPP gives access to private capital but exposes GTC to traffic shocks when it carries debt. [2]
  • SES2+ (Regulation (EU) 2024/2803) was adopted in October 2024 and took effect 1 December 2024. [3][4]
  • SES launched in 2004 with four targets: 10× safety, 3× capacity, half the ATM fees, 10% less environmental impact per flight. [5]
  • Common Project 1 mandates the first SESAR solutions, the foundation for trajectory-based operations. [6]

Still to find out

Four open questions.

  • How regulators set route charges. The EU caps GTC's price, so "customers want it cheaper" works differently here.
  • Fuel and cost saved per flight under trajectory-based routing, to estimate payback on the $800M.
  • What worked and failed in similar rollouts, such as France's 4-Flight.
  • How German ATC unions behave in transitions, and how other providers avoided strikes.
Our argument

Lead the change instead of reacting to it.

  • Phase the rollout. Start in the busiest sectors, run old and new systems side by side, finish in 5 years rather than 10.
  • Hire and retrain now. Training takes 3 years. Bring unions in early so there are no strikes.
  • Market on results. Publish fuel saved and delay minutes cut, then sell those numbers to airlines as the market opens.

Backing

The upgrade is affordable, and waiting is the expensive option.

$2,716M PPP capital

The upgrade is 29% of the $2,716M GTC has raised as a PPP. We'll show how the PPP structure and partnerships fund the rollout.

10-year rollout · done 2036

A slow rollout delays every benefit by up to 5 years. Delay reductions can be pitched to airlines as savings against the €16.1B Europe lost to ATC delays.

Conceptual. A SWOT built from the case challenges and footnotes, and a stakeholder map: airlines, passengers, airports, government, shareholders and environmental groups each want something different, and the plan answers each one.

Ethical. Safety is non-negotiable; SES itself puts it ahead of fuel savings. Shorter routes cut fuel burn and emissions. Current controllers are retrained, not replaced. Government investment makes GTC accountable to taxpayers as well as shareholders.
